Homeschool Planner Printable 2026 — Free Weekly Template for Families

Boxed homeschool planners assume your year goes exactly to plan. It won't. Here's a free weekly homeschool planner you print on demand — and why undated wins for families.

Why homeschool families need a different kind of weekly planner

Homeschooling breaks every assumption a regular planner makes. You're not managing one schedule — you're juggling two, three, four kids, each with their own subjects, pace, and bad days. A dentist appointment doesn't just shift your afternoon; it shifts language arts for three children at once. And a "sick day" can erase a whole week of careful pen-work in a dated planner.

That's why so many homeschool parents end up printing their planner pages instead of buying a bound one. A homeschool planner printable gives you something a $40 spiral-bound planner never can: the freedom to restart the week without wasting pages. Monday fell apart? Print a fresh sheet. Field trip day? Print a one-column version and just log what happened.

What a homeschool weekly planner needs (that generic planners miss)

Before you print anything, check the template against this list — these are the features that decide whether the planner survives week three of your term:

  1. Seven equal day columns. Homeschooling doesn't stop on Saturday — co-ops, sports, and field trips live on weekends. Saturday and Sunday deserve the same space as Wednesday.
  2. A "top 3 goals" box above the days. Not "finish math book." Three real anchors: "finish fractions unit," "science fair project draft," "read-aloud 4x." If the week implodes, the goals still hold.
  3. Room for multiple kids per day. Either one planner page per child, or one family page with subjects stacked per column. Both work — pick one and stay consistent (more below).
  4. An optional habit row. Morning basket, independent reading, instrument practice, chores — homeschool habits track beautifully in a row of checkboxes at the bottom of the page. Our weekly planner with habit tracker covers this in depth.
  5. A notes box. For the things that don't fit a column: "library books due Thursday," "order dissection kit," "co-op enrollment closes Friday."
  6. A4 and US Letter. Homeschool families are everywhere — most free printables are Letter-only, which prints with ugly margins on A4.

Undated beats dated for homeschooling — here's why

A dated planner quietly assumes you'll do school on the dates it printed. Homeschool reality: flu week in October, three weeks off for a new baby, a road trip that becomes a geography unit. In a dated planner, those are dead pages you paid for. Undated, they're just... weeks you didn't print.

Undated also fixes the "we're behind" feeling. If your dated planner shows you two weeks behind by November, the planner itself becomes a guilt object. With an undated template, there is no behind — there's only this week. Write the dates yourself, skip weeks freely, and pick up exactly where you left off.

One page per kid, or one page for the whole family?

Per-kid pages — best for independent readers

Each child gets their own sheet for the week. This works beautifully once kids can read: they own their page, check off their own subjects, and you can see at a glance who's stuck. It also scales — four kids means four sheets, not four overlapping lines in one column.

A family page — best for one-room-schoolhouse style

If you do most subjects together (read-alouds, history, science, morning basket), one family page with per-kid rows inside each day column is simpler. Fewer sheets on the fridge, one planning session. The catch: individual work like math needs its own line per child, so keep handwriting small.

Most families settle on a hybrid: one family page for shared subjects plus a slim per-kid page for independent work. Print both from the same generator so the layout stays consistent.

A 15-minute Sunday routine for your homeschool week

The planner only works if filling it out takes less than a TV episode. Here's the routine that fits on one page:

  1. Friday evening (2 min): glance back at the finished week. Write one note in the box — what worked, what to shift.
  2. Sunday evening (10 min): print the week's pages (one per kid, or one family page). Fill the goals box first, then pencil subjects into day columns, darkest subjects in the morning slots.
  3. Hang it where school happens (1 min): fridge, school-room wall, or the front of the morning binder. Out of sight is out of use.
  4. Each morning (2 min): check off what's done. That's it — the checkboxes are the whole administrative overhead of your day.
