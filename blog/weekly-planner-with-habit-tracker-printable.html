Weekly Planner with Habit Tracker Printable — Free Template

Two pages. Two habits to keep up. Two things to forget. Here's why planning and habit tracking belong on the same sheet — and a free template that puts them there.

The problem with keeping them separate

Most people who try weekly planning and habit tracking end up with two documents: a weekly planner for goals and appointments, and a separate habit tracker — maybe a printable, maybe a phone app, maybe that notebook you bought and abandoned in March.

This sounds fine until Tuesday rolls around. You sit down to plan your day and reach for your weekly planner. That's the page with your three big goals and your meeting schedule. You fill it out. Close it. The habit tracker? It's either on a different sheet at the bottom of your binder, or it's an app you'll "check later." You don't check later. By Friday, you've neither tracked your habits nor looked at the planner again.

They fail separately because they live separately. A weekly planner with a built-in habit tracker solves this with one simple design choice: everything you need for the week is on one side of one piece of paper.

What a combined layout actually gives you

Less friction

One page means one place to open. When you check in on your goals in the morning, the habit trackers are right there. When you cross off a habit at night, you're already staring at your weekly plan — which means you might actually review it. No app-swapping, no binder-flipping, no "I'll do it later."

Visible progress = better decisions

A habit tracker at the bottom of your weekly sheet gives you a visual pulse. If you're tracking "walk 20 minutes" and "read 10 pages," by Wednesday you can see that walking is slipping. That information sits directly below your Thursday to-do list — so when you're deciding whether to say yes to an after-work drink, you're making that call with your habit progress in mind, not in a separate app you might not open.

This is the same logic behind why weekly planners beat daily ones for decision-making: the information you need to say "no" or "not this week" needs to be where you're already making plans, not somewhere else.

One review on Friday

If you keep planning and tracking separate, you probably review neither consistently. Combine them, and the Friday check-in becomes automatic. You flip to the page, scan the goals (did I hit them?), scan the habit row (how many boxes did I tick?), and you're done — two reviews in ten seconds, not two separate rituals you'll skip.

What the template should include

A good weekly planner with a habit tracker needs five components:

  1. Week label — the dates line at the top, so you know which week this is.
  2. Top 3 goals — three boxes above the day columns. Not ten, not five. Three. The ones that matter.
  3. Seven day columns — each day gets a column, wide enough for 3–5 items. Landscape layout works best.
  4. Habit row at the bottom — seven checkboxes per habit, one row per habit. Keep it to 4–6 habits max; more than that dilutes everything.
  5. Notes box — a small area below the habit row for anything that doesn't fit a column or a checkbox.

Layout: columns or rows?

The standard landscape layout (seven columns across) is the most practical for a combined planner. You've got room for goals at the top, seven day columns in the middle, and the habit row stretching across the bottom. Everything flows left to right, just like your week does.

If you prefer portrait orientation — maybe you keep the sheet in a notebook or binder — a vertical layout with seven stacked days still works. The habit row runs across the full width at the bottom. It's slightly narrower, but it fits in standard notebooks without folding.

Both layouts are available in our free weekly planner generator with the built-in habit row. Pick landscape if you want maximum writing room, portrait if you need it in a binder.

How to set it up in 15 minutes (Sunday night)

  1. Print your undated weekly planner with habit tracker.
  2. Fill in the week's dates at the top.
  3. Write your Top 3 goals. Specific outcomes, not vague tasks.
  4. Drop fixed commitments into each day's column — classes, meetings, appointments.
  5. Assign each goal to a day (not the due date, the day you'll work on it).
  6. Pick 4 habits to track this week. Write them in the habit row. Start with the same ones every week until they stick.
  7. Tape it somewhere visible. Desk. Fridge. Notebook cover. Anywhere but a backpack.

That's it. No app setup, no accounts, no notifications to configure. One page on a Sunday evening.

Should you keep a separate tracker too?

For most people, no. The weekly habit row is enough to establish the feedback loop. A separate habit tracker printable makes sense once you've been tracking consistently for a month or two and want to zoom out — monthly views are great for maintenance, but terrible for building new habits. Start with the weekly row. Keep it simple.
